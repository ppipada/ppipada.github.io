Reading towards some clarity

This plan was put together with inputs from some friends over a period of time and after a long conversation with an LLM. I haven’t read all these books yet, and the order may change as I go along. I have already read some Suzuki, Vivekananda and Krishnamurti, but that is not what decides the order below. The idea is to let the understanding build slowly slowly, with one set of questions leading into the next. This is not a course or an academic exercise.

What I am looking for

I want to understand Bhāratīya darśana (Indian philosophy), particularly its ways of looking at the mind, dharma (duty), ethics and ordinary life. Putting all of this into a religion box does not help me much. Neither does presenting it as a collection of ancient versions of whatever self-help idea is currently popular.

I am more interested in the questions underneath. What makes an action right? What do I mean when I say that something is my dharma? How much of what I think is actually understood, and how much is just familiar? Why does a small thing sometimes occupy so much space in the mind?

The mind interests me more than lifestyle advice. But I don’t think I can separate the mind from how I work, speak, treat people or handle responsibilities. If the understanding does not touch any of these things, I am not sure what I would have gained.

I also want to become calmer and less anxious. There is a lot of noise around. I hope this helps me cancel that out a bit. In one of my earlier posts about blunt and direct communication, I wrote about clarity being the desired outcome. That is still important to me. But being clear about something and getting dragged into every possible argument about it are different things.

I would like to get better at separating them.

The kind of reading

Suzuki’s Zen Mind, Beginner’s Mind worked quite well for me. It was a bit circular in places, but it stayed with the questions. The writing was direct, and it gave me space to think about the attitude with which I approach things. I want more reading of this kind, where an explanation gives me something to examine.

A difficult idea can need a lot of explanation. What I try to avoid are things where a simple idea is spread over a large number of pages, with stories and motivational language doing most of the work. I find that quite frustrating. I am fine with spending longer on a dense book if there is something there to understand.

The preference here is for explanations grounded in the traditions themselves. The disagreements are part of the interest too. What did one approach find unsatisfactory in another? What did it keep, and what did it discard? Removing all of that to arrive at a comforting “everything is the same” would defeat much of the purpose.

Some of the books contain claims about Īśvara (God), punarjanma (rebirth), mokṣa (liberation) or things that I don’t really care about. I don’t have to accept those claims to understand an argument. Where an argument depends on them, that is something to notice.

The order below is meant to let one set of questions prepare the ground for the next. It is not chronological. A later book is also not supposed to defeat an earlier one and become the final answer. There is no requirement to agree with a book by the time it is finished.

The structure is fairly detailed because I want to be able to come back to this and remember why a book was included. Most probably there will be gaps between the readings.

The reading order

1. Getting a map

Book: Outlines of Indian Philosophy, M. Hiriyanna.

Aim: Get familiar with the main questions and the different ways of approaching them.

Hiriyanna gives a connected account of the main darśanas (schools of thought), including Vedānta, Yoga, Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, Bauddha, Jaina and Cārvāka (materialist) positions. There is historical material too, but remembering that history is not the main purpose here.

Why here: Words like mind, self, knowledge and freedom keep appearing in this area. Their meanings change across the books. Starting with an overview should make those changes easier to follow and reduce the need to keep looking up the background.

What to look for:

  • What each approach means by mind and self.
  • How it decides whether something is known.
  • What problem it is trying to solve.
  • Where its answers differ from the others.

What to carry forward: Enough familiarity to describe the main positions in ordinary language. The names will probably take care of themselves as they recur in the later reading.

2. Ethics before feeling better

Book: Classical Indian Ethical Thought: A Philosophical Study of Hindu, Jaina and Bauddha Morals, K. N. Tiwari.

Aim: Understand the reasons offered for ethical conduct.

This is the main ethics book in the plan. It brings Hindu, Jaina and Bauddha moral thought together for comparison.

Why here: Personal peace can easily become the only thing being pursued in this type of reading. Everyday responsibilities need attention early on. Avoiding a difficult conversation can bring relief. That does not establish that avoiding it was right.

What to look for:

  • Intention, consequences, dharma and character.
  • Conflicts between responsibilities.
  • The place of desire and restraint.
  • How conduct is connected with karma and liberation.
  • Different expectations for householders and renunciants.

What to carry forward: An account of how these traditions justify conduct, including what they expect from someone living an ordinary life with work, family and other commitments.

3. Work and what gets attached to it

Book: Karma-yoga, Swami Vivekananda.

Aim: Look at motives, attachment and the habits formed through work.

Work brings plenty of opportunities to examine these things. Recognition, disappointment, competition, resentment and the need to establish competence can occupy a large part of an activity. Sometimes the actual work gets very little attention in comparison.

Why here: This follows the broader ethical questions with a closer look at doing things. Duties have to be carried out in accordance with dharma, results arrive, other people respond, and the next piece of work is already waiting.

What to look for:

  • How repeated actions form character.
  • What is involved in calling something dharma.
  • The motives behind service.
  • Dependence on recognition and results.

What to carry forward: Particular motives that are easy to miss while occupied with a task. The next disappointment or unappreciated effort should provide enough material to revisit the book.

4. Attention without another progress chart

Book: Zen Mind, Beginner’s Mind, Shunryu Suzuki.

Aim: Examine the attitude brought to practice.

An attempt to become calmer can itself become another thing to perform well at. Then there is comparison, evaluation, disappointment and the feeling of not progressing fast enough. Much of the same activity has simply moved into a different setting.

Why here: Suzuki gives attention to this tendency before the reading moves further into methods of mental training. His talks return to ordinary activities and to what happens while trying to practise.

What to look for:

  • Beginner’s mind.
  • Attention during ordinary activity.
  • Expectations about progress.
  • What Suzuki means by a gaining idea.

What to carry forward: A closer look at the expectations entering this reading and any practice around it. The constant checking of whether something is working can itself take up a lot of attention.

5. What actually supports a claim?

Book: The Nyāya-sūtra: Selections with Early Commentaries, translated by Matthew R. Dasti and Stephen H. Phillips.

Aim: Examine how knowledge is established and arguments are tested.

This is an edited selection from larger works. The intention is to read the complete published book.

Why here: By this point, the reading has introduced quite a few claims about conduct and mental activity. Nyaya provides a detailed investigation of pramāṇas (means of valid knowledge), including perception, inference, testimony and reasoning. It also has obvious relevance to the information encountered online.

A claim can be annoying and still be correct. It can be reassuring and still be wrong.

What to look for:

  • Reasons for trusting a source.
  • How an inference is supported.
  • Doubt and error.
  • The purposes served by different kinds of debate.

What to carry forward: The ability to point to the part of an argument that needs examination. That might be the evidence, an assumption, or the step connecting the evidence to the conclusion.

6. How a difficult experience becomes more difficult

Book: What the Buddha Taught, Walpola Rahula.

Aim: Understand the Buddhist explanation of dukkha (suffering) and how it is sustained.

Someone says something unpleasant. There is a reaction. Then there may be an interpretation, a defence, an imagined reply, and a much longer story about what the whole thing means. That can occupy the mind long after the conversation has ended.

Why here: Rahula gives a connected Theravāda account of dukkha, sīla (ethical conduct) and bhāvanā (mental cultivation). The earlier reading should help in following its arguments and comparing them with other explanations.

What to look for:

  • Dukkha, taṇhā (craving) and upādāna (attachment).
  • Anicca (impermanence) and the khandhas (aggregates).
  • Anattā (not-self).
  • Sīla and bhāvanā.

What to carry forward: The sequence of a reaction in an actual incident. What was felt, what was wanted, what was resisted, and what kept returning afterwards? These are things that can be examined during an ordinary day.

7. A more deliberate account of mental discipline

Book: Rāja-yoga, Swami Vivekananda.

Aim: Study a structured approach to attention, concentration and mental habits.

Vivekananda combines explanatory lectures with his presentation of Patañjali’s Yoga-sūtras. His Vedānta interpretation also enters the explanation.

Why here: Suzuki and Rahula provide useful points of comparison. Questions about effort, discipline and the effects of practice can now be followed through another system in more detail.

What to look for:

  • Vṛttis (mental activity) and saṃskāras (habitual tendencies).
  • Dhāraṇā (concentration) and abhyāsa (sustained practice).
  • Vairāgya (dispassion).
  • The account of citta (mind).
  • How the effects of practice are explained.

What to carry forward: A working understanding of the terms and the proposed process of training. Claims about physiology or extraordinary capacities need separate assessment.

The physical practices also need care. Reading alone is not sufficient preparation for advanced breath retention.

8. The limits of a judgment

Book: Studies in Jaina Philosophy, Nathmal Tatia.

Aim: Understand Jaina accounts of knowledge, karma and spiritual discipline.

Tiwari provides the comparative ethical foundation. Tatia gives more space to the ideas behind the Jaina position.

Why here: Several explanations of mind and conduct have now been introduced. Jain philosophy brings particular attention to naya (standpoint): what can be said about a subject from a particular position, and what else needs to be considered.

This is relevant to how quickly a single statement or action online becomes a complete judgment about a person.

What to look for:

  • Naya and the scope of a description.
  • The influence of kaṣāyas (passions) and rāga (attachment).
  • Individual living selves (jīvas) and karmic bondage.
  • Saṃyama (restraint) in thought and conduct.

What to carry forward: An explanation of what considering multiple standpoints actually involves. “Everyone has their own truth” is too vague to do much with. The details of how Jaina thinkers handle a claim are the interesting part here.

9. Self and reality

Book: Jñāna-yoga, Swami Vivekananda.

Aim: Follow the Vedānta account of ātman (self), reality and mokṣa.

The main ideas include ātman, Brahman (ultimate reality), māyā (appearance) and an underlying unity. Vivekananda develops these through questions about the person and changing experience.

Why here: The earlier books give alternatives against which to examine this account. Terms like self and ātman and consciousness should by now bring several possible explanations to mind.

What to look for:

  • The real and apparent person.
  • The subject of experience.
  • Arguments for nonduality.
  • The connection Vivekananda makes between these ideas and conduct.

What to carry forward: The reasoning behind the conclusions. If thoughts can be observed, what follows about the observer? How does the argument reach something unchanging, or one consciousness? These steps need enough time to be understood.

10. Questioning the method too

Book: Freedom from the Known, J. Krishnamurti, edited by Mary Lutyens.

Aim: Examine dependence on explanations, methods and psychological authority.

Krishnamurti questions conditioning, comparison, fear and the pursuit of an ideal self. He also questions the effort to bring about change through a prescribed method.

Why here: After reading several structured accounts, there are actual methods and explanations to consider alongside his objections. It should be easier to identify what he is challenging and whether the challenge holds.

What to look for:

  • Psychological authority.
  • Comparison and fear.
  • The pursuit of an ideal self.
  • His account of the observer and the observed.

What to carry forward: Questions about the reading itself. Which explanations have become comfortable? Which are difficult to question? Krishnamurti’s answers belong in that examination too.

A few questions to keep around

I don’t want to turn the reading into note-taking for its own sake. But writing things down generally helps me build clarity, so I will probably keep a small amount of it.

After a book, these seem worth recording:

  • What did it help me notice?
  • Which argument did I find convincing, and why?
  • What did it ask me to accept without establishing it?
  • Where did it disagree with an earlier book?
  • Would taking it seriously change anything in my conduct?
  • What am I still unsure about?

Cārvāka thought is also relevant throughout this reading. The surviving evidence is often indirect and needs care, but the skeptical questions are important.

What was actually observed? What was inferred? What assumptions connect the two? Could another explanation fit the same experience?

I would particularly like to avoid treating the usefulness of a practice as proof of its entire philosophy.

For ordinary incidents, the notes can be even simpler. What happened, what I know about it, what I felt, what story I added, and what response is actually needed.

The response may sometimes be a difficult conversation or a clear disagreement. Calmness cannot just mean finding a philosophical reason to avoid those things.

What I hope changes

I am not sure how much of this will carry into daily life. Reading about something and being able to act differently when it matters are very different things.

Still, there are a few changes I would consider useful.

Not feeling obliged to answer every provocation would be a useful start. I’d like to become better at leaving questions unresolved instead of immediately filling the gap with confidence. Understanding an opposing position without making it sound stupid is another thing worth working on. And perhaps not requiring every piece of work to produce recognition.

I would also like the time spent reading to feel less like processing another stream of information. Some space to stay with an idea, without immediately looking for the next one, would be good.

There is a fairly obvious failure mode here. I could finish the books, acquire a larger vocabulary, and continue doing the same things with better explanations for them. I don’t want to confuse that with understanding.

Most probably the order will change as I go through it. Some books may be less useful than expected. Others may need another reading much later. That is fine.

For now, writing down the plan has helped make the intention clearer. The next part is to read slowly and see whether any of that clarity survives outside the reading itself.
